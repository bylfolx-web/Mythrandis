They had stayed at the flat rocks past the mill until Dusty's fingertips puckered white. Auriel folded her cream silk gown over a sun-warm rock and waded in wearing only her shift, the thin linen gone sheer the moment the lake touched it, clinging to the heavy curve of her breasts and the round swell of her hips. Dusty fixed his eyes on the mill wheel and kept them there until she called his name twice.

She stood waist-deep in the warm shallows, gold hair floating out around her in a fan, and slid one palm flat beneath his back. "It wants to hold you up," she said. "Stop arguing with it. Let it."

He sank four times. The fifth time he lay back with the water filling his ears and the sky stretched blue and empty above him, and her hand slid away from his spine, and he floated.

By the time the sun had crossed the top of the sky, the bread and pear from Rinnie's tray had burned away to nothing, and his stomach growled loud enough that Auriel heard it over the water. They crossed back over the bridge with their hair dripping on the stone. Pell waved them through without a word, his eyes on the far end of the bridge. Dusty ate three plates in the nearest dining hall while Auriel picked at a plum, lake water beading off the ends of her hair and pattering on the bench.

Then she led him down past the western lawns to a cove where the sand shelved slow and the bottom stayed in sight a long way out, and they swam until the willow shadows stretched across the water. He swam badly. His kicks threw up more spray than distance, and twice he swallowed half the lake and came up coughing while Auriel laughed and slapped the surface. By the last hour he could cross the cove from one willow to the other without touching bottom. He did it three times in a row to be sure.

***

The lake path ran along the shore beneath the willows, packed earth and crushed white shell crunching under their boots. The sun sat low and swollen over the water, its light laid across the lake in a road of hammered copper that ran from the far haze to the reeds at their feet. Midges hung in the gold over the shallows. Somewhere out on the water a fish jumped and the rings spread copper and black and copper again.

They walked slowly. Auriel's gown had dried stiff in places and soft in others, and her hair hung down her back in damp, heavy ropes that darkened the silk between her shoulder blades. She swung her boots from two fingers by their laces. Dusty carried his blazer over one shoulder, his shirt still damp at the collar, the blue tie stuffed in his pocket for the second day running.

His shoulders ached from the water, a clean, loose ache. His belly was full. Rinnie had laughed out loud at him that morning, bright and loud, and the sound of it was still in his ears.

He realized he was humming. It was his father's song, the one he used to sing over the boot last in the room above the cooper's shop, flat on every high note. Dusty had not hummed it in eight years. He did not stop.

Auriel tilted her head and looked up at him, her golden eyes narrowing. "Is that a song?"

"It was supposed to be."

"It's terrible." She bumped his arm with her shoulder. "Keep going."

He kept going. She hummed along a half-beat behind him, wrong in all the same places, and the sun slid lower.

The scream came from beyond the bend ahead, where the path curved out of sight behind a stand of willows. A girl's voice, high and torn. A boy's voice tangled with it, then choked off.

Auriel dropped her boots and ran.

Dusty's feet stayed where they were. In the Greys, screaming meant someone was already bleeding, and the people who ran toward it came back bleeding too, or didn't come back. The rule lived in his legs, and they would not move. He watched Auriel's cream silk flash between the willow trunks, bare feet slapping the shell path, gold hair streaming.

He swore under his breath and went after her.

He came around the bend into the open. The willows fell back from a broad stretch of pale sand that sloped down into the lake. Two students were floundering up out of the water, a boy and a girl, both in their uniforms, soaked to the chest. The water churned white around their legs.

Behind them, two long shapes rose out of the lake.

They were thin as a girl's wrist and clear as lamp glass, with a faint blue running through their length the way the veins ran through starwick leaves. They moved without hurry. They came out of the water and kept coming, longer than any creature Dusty had ever seen, the copper light of the sun bending through them.

The boy reached the dry sand first. One of the clear lengths whipped forward and wound around his throat, twice, and lifted him to his toes. His hands clawed at it and slid off.

The girl spun. Blue light flared around her hands, and a spike of ice the length of a spear cracked into being and shot at the tentacle. It struck dead on and bounced, spinning end over end into the sand.

The boy's kicking slowed. His arms dropped. Dusty watched the color run out of his face, out of his lips, out of the hands that hung at his sides.

The second tentacle snapped around the girl's raised wrist.

She screamed. The blue glow on her hands went out, all at once.

She shook her arm. Blue light flickered at her fingertips and guttered, flickered and guttered, and Dusty watched her mouth fall open and her eyes go white all the way around. She was trying to call it back, and it would not come.

Dusty stood at the edge of the sand with his chest heaving.

The boy had gone pale. The girl's glow had died the moment it touched her. It was drinking them, the way a leech drank. It was drinking their mana.

His own flame pressed hot behind his fingernails. It had never once run out. He had held it all night, every night, for years. He had never seen the bottom of it, and Valia, with all her gems, had not found one.

But he had never fought anything that ate light.

"Dusty!" Auriel was already halfway down the sand. She flung an arm back toward the girl without looking at him. "Her! Help her! I've got the boy!"

She turned on the tentacle choking the boy, and the air around her went hot.

Dusty ran.

Heat rolled off Auriel as he passed her, the same heat he had felt on the north shore, pressing against his side like an open oven door. Then he was at the girl. Up close she was small, dark-haired, freckled, her blazer violet and her lips going gray at the edges. She was hauling backward on her own arm with her whole weight, heels dug into the wet sand, and sobbing.

He brought up both hands. The flame came at once, two of them, one in each palm, black haze rolling off them, orange at their hearts, the silver edge drawn fine as a hair. He thrust them at the clear length wrapped around her wrist.

The tentacle smoked. A smell like a burnt eel skin on a hot grate filled his nose. It loosed her wrist all at once, and she tumbled backward into the sand, scrambled up, and ran for the willows, cradling her arm against her chest, her face pale as milk.

The tentacle came for him.

It wound around his left wrist faster than he could see it move. It was cold. It was colder than the north-shore lake closing over his head, and the cold went into the skin and then under it.

Then his mana began to leave him.

He felt it go. A steady pull at his wrist, the way the violet light in Valia's lab had pulled, but this did not let up. It drank. He felt his reserve tilt and slosh like water in a bucket being carried too fast, and the level dropped. And dropped. And kept dropping.

He had never known the feel of less. His knees went weak beneath him.

He gritted his teeth and called the flame again. It came smaller this time, but it came. He did not throw it. He did what he had done in the healing pen with Rinnie in his mind. He pictured it. He pictured the flame inside the clear length, sitting in its blue vein where the cold lived.

The flame was there.

A patch of the tentacle as long as his forearm turned black, all at once, the clear glass of it gone dead and dark as coal. The tentacle convulsed. It ripped free of his wrist, leaving a ring of white, bloodless skin, and whipped back toward the water, the black patch dripping.

Dusty swayed. His mana was still there. Less of it, and coming back slowly. He looked at his own hand. It was shaking.

He turned toward Auriel.

She stood at the waterline with gold light pouring off her skin, so bright it threw a second shadow from her across the sand. Her hands moved through the air before her, sweeping and pointing, and above the boy a sword swung in answer to them. It was longer than Auriel was tall, gold from point to pommel, and it hung in the air with no hand on its hilt. It came down on the tentacle wrapped around the boy's throat, again and again, and rang off it every time as if striking an anvil.

The boy hung limp in its grip. His skin had gone from pale to gray, the gray of the ash heaps behind the tanneries, and his lips were blue.

The tentacle held him out over the deep water.

Dusty looked at that water. The sand shelved away beneath the surface, and past the first few paces the lake went from gold-brown to black. He had learned to float that morning. He had crossed one willow cove three times in a row.

He breathed out, and took a step into the lake.

Shadows moved behind him.

They rushed past him on either side, low and fast, and became women. Three Maids, black dresses and white aprons, long black lace gloves, ran past him into the lake and did not sink. Their stiletto heels struck the surface and it held them, each step throwing up a small, neat splash, as if they ran across wet flagstones.

The first had swords of ice circling her, thin as reeds and blue-white, a dozen of them spiraling around her body as she ran. The second had rocks following her, great gray boulders from the shoreline lifting and falling into her wake like hounds at heel. The third raised her lace-gloved hands, and orbs of purple light bloomed around her fingers, some the size of grapes, some the size of a man's head, drifting where she pointed.

They reached the boy together.

The boulders came down on the tentacle with a crack that Dusty felt in his teeth, crushing it flat against the surface of the lake. The ice swords drove down on either side of it, a dozen at once, and pinned it there, not piercing, holding it in place like the bars of a cage. The purple orbs sank into the clear flesh one after another and dissolved inside it.

The tentacle turned a sickly yellow-brown, the color of an old bruise, from the boulders all the way down into the water.

It tore free and whipped back beneath the surface, the way the other one had gone. The lake swallowed it without a ripple.

The boy dropped.

The Maid with the ice swords caught him before he touched the water, one arm under his knees and one behind his shoulders, and turned. All three of them came racing back across the lake.

Straight at Dusty.

Their faces were set and pale, their eyes fixed on him, the ice swords still circling, the boulders still riding the air behind them. Dusty took a step back out of the water. Then another. His fists came up. His flame flickered in his palms, small and spent.

"Dusty!"

Auriel was running at him, too. Her face had gone white, her mouth stretched wide, her hands flinging wildly through the air. The gold sword wheeled above her and came screaming toward him point first.

He understood it all at once. They were not looking at him. They were looking past him.

He spun.

The blue tentacle had come up out of the sand behind him, the sand still sliding off it. Its tip was burned black where his flame had been, and blue fluid dripped from the wound in thick, slow strings. It reared over his head. In front of it hung a curve of ice like a half moon, thick and heavy at the back and thinning to an edge that caught the sun and burned white.

It came down.

A boulder slammed into the tentacle from the side a heartbeat before it struck, knocking it off its line.

It did not knock it far enough.

The ice edge took him at the shoulder. The pain was white and enormous, and he heard himself scream. Then the pain stopped, and in its place was an absence, a cold, hollow nothing where the pain should have been.

The tentacle drew back. The thick end of the ice swung with it and cracked against his temple.

The sun went out.

He was falling, and the copper light was gone, and the sand never came. The last thing that followed him down into the dark was a girl, screaming.
